Frequently Asked Questions in Egyptian Criminal Cases: Arrest, Investigation, Pretrial Detention, Opposition, and Settlement

By Counselor Dr. Hassan Abdel Moneim Hassan, Attorney before the Court of Cassation

Frequently Asked Questions in Egyptian Criminal Cases: Arrest, Investigation, Pretrial Detention, Opposition, and Settlement

This article is not a theoretical study but direct answers to the questions people ask us in criminal matters, from the moment of arrest or summons until judgment. We have collected them as short questions, and each answer rests on the well-known legal provision governing it at the time of writing. It is general information and does not replace review of your case by a lawyer.

1. I, or a relative, have been arrested. What do I do?

Ask for a lawyer immediately, and do not sign any statement or record before he attends. The law requires that the arrested person’s statements be heard at once and that he be sent to the Public Prosecution within 24 hours (Criminal Procedure Code, Art. 36), and the accused may remain silent. If you do not know where your relative is, ask the police station of his area or the Prosecution, and notify a lawyer to follow up.

2. Do I need a lawyer at the investigation?

In felonies and misdemeanors punishable by mandatory imprisonment the investigator may not interrogate the accused or confront him with others except after inviting his lawyer, and the Prosecution appoints a lawyer if he has none, unless the matter is urgent for fear of losing evidence (Art. 124). Otherwise a lawyer’s attendance remains useful, because much that follows is built on what is recorded in the investigation record.

3. Pretrial detention: what is it and how long can it last?

It is a measure taken during the investigation, not a penalty, used where there is fear of flight, tampering with evidence, or influencing witnesses. Periods are renewed by successive orders within maximum limits set by the current law: 6 months in misdemeanors, 18 months in felonies, and two years where the possible penalty is death or life imprisonment (Art. 143). The detention order or its renewal may be challenged.

4. Can I be released on bail?

Yes. The investigating authority or the court may release a person in pretrial detention on bail or under other measures, such as residing in a specified place or appearing at specified times. Release is requested by a reasoned application and depends on the circumstances of the case, the gravity of the charge, and guarantees of appearance.

5. A police report has been filed against me. Should I go to the station?

If you are summoned, consult a lawyer before attending and go with him if possible; note the report number and prepare your documents. Failing to attend after being summoned may lead to arrest and bringing, or to harsher measures. Being told “So-and-so filed a report against you” does not yet mean you are charged in a criminal case; the report may be archived or referred to the Prosecution.

6. A judgment in absentia was issued against me. What do I do?

In misdemeanors, opposition is available within 10 days of service (Art. 398), and appeal within 10 days of the pronouncement (Art. 406). The deadlines are short and depend on the type of judgment and its service, so consult a lawyer at once. For the other ways of challenging a judgment (cassation and reopening) see our article on the difference between opposition, appeal, and cassation, and for the last stage of appeal see our article on the criminal cassation appeal.

7. Can the case be settled?

Settlement is allowed in specified misdemeanors listed exhaustively (Art. 18 bis and following) and in some economic offenses under special provisions, and its effect differs by stage of the action (before the Prosecution, before the court, or after judgment). For felonies the rule is that it does not extinguish the action, although a waiver by the victim may carry practical weight in the Prosecution’s and the court’s assessment. Present your matter to a lawyer to confirm whether the law allows settlement in it.

8. Complaint, police report, case: what is the difference?

A complaint notifies the authorities of an incident; the report is the official record of what was said and what happened; the Prosecution investigates if it sees merit, then archives or refers to the court, at which point it becomes a “case.” A report against you does not mean a case has been brought against you, but it must not be ignored.

9. Common mistakes that complicate your position

  • Signing statements or records before reading them in full or before the lawyer attends.
  • Withholding a fact from your lawyer out of embarrassment, so that it appears later in the papers and its effect is hard to repair.
  • Contacting, pressuring, or threatening the other party, turning your position from defender into accused of a new act.
  • Relying on “someone told me” instead of reading the judgment or decision itself.
  • Letting the opposition and appeal deadlines pass while waiting for a settlement or mediation.
  • Deleting messages and photos or hiding evidence, which may be construed against you.

A note on procedure: the new Criminal Procedure Law No. 174 of 2025 has been issued, but its entry into force is postponed to 2027/10/1, so the procedures in this article rest on the current Criminal Procedure Code, Law No. 150 of 1950, for as long as it remains in force, and the article numbers cited are its numbers.

A Question Worth Knowing the Answer To

I, or a relative, have been arrested in Egypt. What is the first thing to do?

Answer: Ask for a lawyer immediately and do not sign any statement or record before he arrives. The law requires that an arrested person’s statements be heard at once and that he be sent to the Public Prosecution within 24 hours (Criminal Procedure Code, Art. 36); in felonies and misdemeanors punishable by mandatory imprisonment, the investigator may not interrogate the accused or confront him with others except after inviting his lawyer, unless the matter is urgent (Art. 124). You may remain silent until your lawyer attends.

What is pretrial detention and what is its maximum length?

Answer: Pretrial detention is a precautionary measure during the investigation, not a penalty, and rests on fear of flight, tampering with evidence, or influencing witnesses. The current law sets maximum limits, among them 6 months in misdemeanors, 18 months in felonies, and two years where the possible penalty is death or life imprisonment (Art. 143). You may challenge the detention order or its renewal and apply for release on bail or on guarantee of residence.
