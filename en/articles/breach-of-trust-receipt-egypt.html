Trust Receipts and Breach of Trust in Egyptian Law: When Is the Signatory Jailed, and How Does He Defend Himself?

By Counselor Dr. Hassan Abdel Moneim Hassan, Attorney before the Court of Cassation

Trust Receipts and Breach of Trust in Egyptian Law: When Is the Signatory Jailed, and How Does He Defend Himself?

One of the most common questions in the criminal courts: “I signed a trust receipt,” or “he took money from me as a trust and won’t return it,” or “someone filed a trust receipt against me.” The answer: not every receipt with the word “trust” on it is an offense, and not everyone who received money and did not return it is a breach of trust. The offense has elements in law, and the true relationship between the parties decides the matter, not the title of the paper. This article explains it for the complainant and the accused alike.

First: What is breach of trust?

Article 341 of the Penal Code punishes anyone who misappropriates, uses, or squanders sums, goods, cash, tickets, or other writings containing an obligation or a discharge, to the detriment of their owners or holders, where they were delivered to him only as a deposit, lease, loan for use, or pledge, or were delivered to him as an agent, paid or unpaid, in order to display, sell, or use them for a specific purpose for the benefit of their owner or another; the penalty is imprisonment. Analytically it has four elements:

ElementMeaningPractical note
A movable item belonging to anotherMovable property, a document, or cash belonging to someone other than the recipientReal estate cannot be the object of this offense
Delivery as a trustDelivery under one of the contracts listed exhaustively in Article 341 (deposit, lease, loan for use, pledge, agency)If delivery was for another reason, such as a loan, a sale, or a partnership, the offense is not made out
Misappropriation, squandering, or useThe recipient deals with the property as owner, denies receiving it, or uses it other than as deliveredMere delay in returning is not enough alone
Criminal intent and harmHe knows the property is not his and intends to take it or harm its ownerThe act must cause harm to the owner or the right-holder

Second: The trust receipt: what is its legal value?

A trust receipt is a paper proving that its signatory received money as a trust, but it is not the offense itself and does not dispense with proving its elements. The Court of Cassation has established that what counts is the true relationship between the parties, not what the paper says: if it appears that the receipt covers a debt, a loan, the price of goods, or a civil obligation, breach of trust is not made out and the money is claimed by a civil action. If, however, it is proved that the signatory actually received the money or the item as a deposit, as an agent, or on loan and then squandered or denied it, the offense is made out. Receipts are often used out of place to pressure a debtor, which is why the accused’s defense usually begins by proving the true nature of the relationship.

If the receipt was signed in blank and was later filled in without the signatory’s knowledge or contrary to what was agreed, the incident may be a different offense, such as forgery or breach of confidence over a paper signed in blank (Penal Code, Art. 340); the characterization varies with the circumstances of delivery and writing and needs careful legal assessment.

Third: How is the offense proved?

In proving non-criminal matters on which the case depends, including the existence of the trust contract, the criminal courts follow the rules of proof laid down by the law governing those matters (Criminal Procedure Code, Art. 225), that is, the civil rules of evidence. Practically, this means that a deposit, agency, or loan contract whose value exceeds the statutory limit for proof by witnesses cannot be proved except by writing or its equivalent, while misappropriation and squandering may be proved by all means. The written document of receipt is therefore the most important evidence in the case.

Fourth: Steps for the complainant (the owner of the money)

  1. First determine whether your incident is breach of trust, fraud, or a civil debt; the correct characterization determines the report and its content.
  2. Prepare the receipt document (the receipt, contract, or correspondence proving delivery as a trust), what proves the value of the property, and what proves your demand for its return and the refusal or denial.
  3. File a report with the Public Prosecution or the police station with the details of the delivery, the reason for the trust, and how the property was squandered or denied; or bring a direct misdemeanor action before the Misdemeanor Court if the accused is known.
  4. Join as a civil claimant to demand return of the property or its value and compensation, before the criminal court as an adjunct to the action or by a separate civil action.
  5. The limitation period of the criminal action in misdemeanors is three years from the offense, interrupted by investigation and trial; do not leave it long.

Fifth: Steps for the accused (against whom the receipt was presented)

  1. Do not ignore a summons or contact the other party with threats or pressure; consult a lawyer before attending or signing any acknowledgment.
  2. Determine the true relationship: did you actually receive something as a trust, or did you sign as security for a debt or a transaction, or in blank? Prepare what proves it (transfers, contracts, messages, witnesses, repayment receipts).
  3. Raise the basic defenses: absence of a trust contract, that the receipt is security for a civil debt, absence of misappropriation and criminal intent, that the money was returned, that a blank signature was later tampered with, or that the action is time-barred.
  4. If a judgment in absentia is issued in the misdemeanor, remember the opposition and appeal deadlines (10 days each, Arts. 398 and 406), and see our article frequently asked questions in criminal cases.
  5. If settlement is possible, present your matter to a lawyer to confirm its conditions and effects at the stage your case has reached.

Sixth: Common mistakes

  • Believing that a paper marked “trust” dispenses with proving the true relationship.
  • Signing a trust receipt in blank or as security for a transaction without writing the reason for delivery, its value, and its date.
  • Filing a breach-of-trust report over a civil debt, which is archived or ends in acquittal and wastes months.
  • Neglecting the defense in the misdemeanor because the accused thinks it is “a simple paper,” and judgments are then issued that are enforced.
  • Paying without obtaining a discharge receipt or getting back the original receipt.
  • Waiting for a final judgment before consulting a lawyer.

A Question Worth Knowing the Answer To

Does signing a trust receipt mean I will be jailed?

Answer: Not necessarily. The receipt alone does not make out the offense of breach of trust: Article 341 of the Penal Code requires that the property was actually delivered as a trust under one of the contracts it lists (deposit, lease, loan for use, pledge, or agency), and that the recipient then misappropriates or squanders it to the owner’s detriment. If the receipt in reality secures a debt, a loan, or a sale price, the dispute is civil and the offense is not made out. The court draws the true nature of the relationship from the circumstances of the case.

What is the difference between breach of trust and fraud?

Answer: In fraud, the offender deceives you by fraudulent means and you hand over your property because of that deceit (Art. 336). In breach of trust you hand him the property voluntarily as a trust, without deceit, and he then squanders or denies it (Art. 341). The difference is fundamental because the elements of each offense and the way they are proved differ, so the incident must be characterized correctly before the report is written.
