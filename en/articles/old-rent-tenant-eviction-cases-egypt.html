When Can a Tenant Be Evicted Under Egypt's New Old-Rent Law No. 164 of 2025?

By Omnia Hany Hussein — Attorney

When Can a Tenant Be Evicted Under Egypt's New Old-Rent Law No. 164 of 2025?

Landlords and tenants in Egypt's "old rent" system keep asking the same questions: can the owner take the flat back now? What if the flat has been locked while I work abroad? Do I lose my old lease if I buy another apartment? When does it all end? The short answer: Article 7 of Law No. 164 of 2025 requires the tenant, or whoever the lease passed to, to vacate in three cases: when the transition period ends (seven years for residential and five for non-residential leases, counted from the law's entry into force), when the premises have been left closed for more than a year without justification, or when the tenant owns another unit usable for the same purpose. The long-standing grounds in Article 18 of Law No. 136 of 1981 continue to apply alongside them.

"Eviction" in everyday speech means vacating by order of a court. An owner cannot remove a tenant on his own.

Which leases does the new law cover?

Under Article 1, Law No. 164 of 2025 applies to premises let for residence, and to premises let to natural persons for non-residential use, that are governed by Laws No. 49 of 1977 and No. 136 of 1981. Leases made under the Civil Code ("new rent") follow their own terms and duration; see our article on lease terms.

The older grounds: Article 18 of Law No. 136 of 1981

Article 7 opens with the words "without prejudice to Article 18 of Law No. 136 of 1981", so the established grounds survive during the transition period. As reported in the press [needs verification against the official text of Article 18], they are:

  • Demolition and repair: full or partial demolition of buildings at risk of collapse, and temporary vacating needed for repair and maintenance.
  • Non-payment of rent: after formal notice by registered letter or bailiff and fifteen days without payment; eviction is not ordered if the tenant pays the rent and costs before the close of pleadings, with special rules for repeated non-payment without a justification the court accepts.
  • Assignment or subletting without the owner's express written permission, outside the cases the law allows.
  • Harmful use established by a final court judgment: using the premises in a way that disturbs others, endangers the building or public health, or for immoral purposes.

How does eviction work in practice?

  1. If the tenant refuses to leave in an Article 7 case, the owner or landlord may ask the urgent-matters judge of the court where the property is located for an eviction order, without prejudice to any claim for compensation.
  2. The tenant may bring a substantive lawsuit, but the law states expressly that filing it does not suspend the judge's order.
  3. Article 18 grounds are normally pursued through an eviction lawsuit before the competent court [needs verification: whether the urgent-matters route also extends to them].
  4. Challenges to the order and their time limits are governed by the Code of Civil Procedure [needs verification of the applicable articles and deadlines].

Is the tenant entitled to alternative housing?

Under Article 8, before the transition period ends the tenant may apply for a State-owned residential or non-residential unit, to rent or to buy, attaching an undertaking to vacate the leased premises once a unit is allocated. The original tenant and spouse have priority, and the Cabinet was to issue the rules within one month of the law's entry into force.

What happens when the transition period ends?

Article 9 repeals Laws No. 49 of 1977, No. 136 of 1981 and No. 6 of 1997 from the day after seven years have passed since the law's entry into force, subject to Article 2. From then on, the general rules govern the relationship.

Common mistakes

  • Owners trying to remove a tenant themselves by changing locks or cutting utilities instead of going to court.
  • Tenants withholding the new rent or the interim amount in the belief that this carries no eviction risk.
  • Leaving premises closed for long periods without keeping proof of the reason, such as an overseas employment contract or medical reports.
  • Letting the flat to others, or leaving it with them, without the owner's written permission.

Specialist legal view

During the transition period Law No. 164 of 2025 runs two regimes side by side: the traditional grounds in Article 18 of Law No. 136 of 1981, and three new grounds in Article 7 that benefit from a faster route through an urgent-matters order. The second and third grounds rest on facts the owner must prove, and the tenant can contest them by showing a justification for the closure or that the owned unit is not suitable for the same purpose. Both sides should prepare their documents early, follow implementing decrees and court rulings on the new law, and check the courts' latest settled position before taking any step.

Frequently asked questions

On what grounds can an old-rent tenant be evicted under the new law?

Article 7 of Law No. 164 of 2025 provides for eviction when the transition period ends (seven years residential, five non-residential), when the premises have been closed for more than a year without justification, or when the tenant owns a unit usable for the same purpose; the grounds in Article 18 of Law No. 136 of 1981 also remain.

Can the owner evict the tenant himself?

No. Eviction requires a court order or judgment; in Article 7 cases the owner applies to the urgent-matters judge of the court where the property is located, and a substantive lawsuit by the tenant does not suspend that order.

When do residential old-rent leases finally end?

Seven years after Law No. 164 of 2025 entered into force, which was the day after its publication in the Official Gazette, unless the parties agree to end the lease earlier (Articles 2 and 10).

Is the tenant entitled to a replacement flat from the State?

Before the transition period ends, the tenant may apply for a State-owned unit to rent or buy, with an undertaking to vacate once it is allocated; the original tenant and spouse have priority (Article 8).
